# Teaching Stance — *A* Way Not *The* Way, Selling Nothing, and Staying Flexible as the Tools Shift

**Source:** Claude Boot Camp planning session, 2026-09-11 — Dave's framing for the eventual front matter.
**Type:** concept
**Verified:** n/a — project decision, not a product claim. `[confirm-this: the Verified axis was designed for factual claims about products; decision cards like this one don't fit it. Worth deciding whether the schema needs a 'decision' value.]`
**Relevant to:** general (front matter for the boot camp / book / deck)

## Content

The stance the material takes toward its reader:

**Why listen to me.** A brief personal framing — how I think, and real things I've built. Some planned, some serendipitous. The serendipitous ones matter just as much, because they're evidence of what happens when you follow a thread instead of a roadmap. The point isn't credentials; it's to make the case that you should build, experiment, follow your own curiosity, and solve your own frictions. If someone leaves having built one small thing for themselves, that's the outcome.

**Not selling anything.** Not the approach, not Claude, not Anthropic. Where a tool doesn't fit a situation, say so.

**Not *the* right way — just *a* way.** Mine. This is the same principle as the teaching format's "the demo is one path, not the path," applied to the whole project: the workflows, the tooling choices, the method itself.

**Stay flexible.** These tools and models change constantly, and even minor updates shift the working dynamics — what was awkward last month is a one-liner now, and habits built around a limitation outlive the limitation. So teach a way of *evaluating and adapting*, not a fixed recipe. Anything written as "always do X" will be wrong within a year. Prefer explaining the underlying constraint, so a reader can tell when it stops applying.

## Why this matters

The stance has a structural consequence, not just a tonal one: it's an argument for the card method itself.

Cards carry their own source dates and `Verified` status, so staleness stays *visible* — you can see that a note was `docs`-verified fourteen months ago against a product that has since changed. Polished prose hides that. Once a claim is smoothed into a chapter, its age and its evidentiary basis disappear, and stale advice reads exactly like current advice.

For a curriculum about fast-moving tools, that's the difference between a document that ages honestly and one that quietly becomes wrong.
